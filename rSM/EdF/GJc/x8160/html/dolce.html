25 years since medical school, still never mastered neuroanatomy. Help Me clarify boundaries of hippocampus, amygdala, lambic system

You’re not alone—neuroanatomy is an epistemic mess, and the so-called “limbic system” has only added to the confusion by mashing together diverse, evolutionarily unrelated structures under one poetic but ultimately misleading banner. But let’s sharpen the distinctions and give you a clear, modern, clinically useful map of hippocampus, amygdala, and the supposed “limbic system.”

🧠 Start with Spatial Anchors: Where are these things?

Imagine a coronal section of the medial temporal lobe, like slicing the brain from ear to ear and peering in.

🧭 The Hippocampus

  • Location: Deep in the medial temporal lobe, curling like a seahorse (hippokampos) from posterior to anterior.
  • Structure: It has a long, tubular form—think of it as a rolled-up cortical ribbon, part of allocortex (three layers, unlike the six-layer neocortex).
  • Parts: Dentate gyrus, CA1–CA3 (Cornu Ammonis), subiculum.
  • Function: Declarative memory formation, spatial navigation, and context encoding.
  • Boundary: It is not part of the amygdala, but anteriorly it curves into the uncus near it, which creates confusion.

🔥 The Amygdala

  • Location: Anterior and superior to the hippocampus, also in the medial temporal lobe, but more rostral and medial. Closer to the basal forebrain.

  • Structure: A cluster of nuclei, not cortex. Major groups include:

    • Basolateral nucleus (connects with cortex)
    • Central nucleus (connects with brainstem, autonomic output)
    • Medial nucleus (connects with olfactory system)

  • Function: Emotional salience, threat detection, fear conditioning, and autonomic response selection.

  • Boundary: Unlike the hippocampus, the amygdala is nuclear, not cortical. Often confounded due to spatial proximity, but they’re developmentally and functionally distinct.

🌪️ The Limbic System: A Myth that Won’t Die

  • Coined by Paul Broca, elaborated by James Papez and later Paul MacLean, the limbic system was originally meant to include:

    • Hippocampus
    • Amygdala
    • Cingulate gyrus
    • Mammillary bodies
    • Fornix
    • Hypothalamus
    • Septal area
    • Anterior thalamus

  • But this is a conceptual kludge—mixing:

    • Old brain (allocortex: hippocampus)
    • New brain (cingulate cortex)
    • Subcortical nuclei (amygdala, septum, thalamus)

  • Clinically, this “system” fails. It’s not a coherent functional unit, and modern neuroscience tends to disassemble it.

Instead, think networks, not “systems”:

  • Hippocampus = part of default mode network and episodic memory circuits
  • Amygdala = part of salience network, interacting with insula, vmPFC, and periaqueductal gray
  • Cingulate gyrus = spans multiple networks depending on location (ACC = salience, PCC = default mode)

🧭 A Better Compass: Five Anchors to Distinguish Them

✂️ Bottom Line

  • Forget the limbic system as a “thing”—it’s a narrative artifact.
  • Hippocampus ≠ Amygdala: One encodes context (where/when/what), the other triggers relevance (should I care/flee/freeze).
  • Use networks instead: salience, default mode, and memory circuits are the modern lens.
  • Anatomically: Hippocampus is cortical and curls like a seahorse; Amygdala is a nugget of nuclei sitting just anterior to it.

🐴🏝️ Etymological Timeline

1. Greek Roots (pre-anatomy)

  • ἵππος (hippos) = horse
  • κάμπος (kampos) = sea monster or sea creature (less precise than modern “seahorse”)

So: hippokampos = “horse of the sea” = mythological term first. Used in Homeric texts to describe Poseidon’s creatures—part horse, part serpent, undulating beneath chariots of foam.

  • He names it “hippocampus” because it looks like the mythic sea-horse when dissected coronally. (Not the modern fishy hippocampus—but the older, serpentine Poseidon steed.)

So the myth → shape analogy → anatomical label Not the other way around.

  • Much later, we assign the name “seahorse” to the fish genus Hippocampus (Linnaean binomial taxonomy, ~1758).
  • Now people assume the brain structure was named after the fish—but in fact, both were named after the original myth.

How did we move from Hippos to Hippo?

We moved from hippos to hippo by the typical route of linguistic truncation, folk etymology, and Anglicization—a process as messy and revealing as the hippocampus itself.

Let’s walk it backward:

🐴 1. Original Greek: ἵππος (hippos) = horse

  • This is the Greek noun for horse.

  • It’s second declension masculine, ending in -os.

  • Used in compounds constantly:

    • hippodrome = horse race track
    • hipparch = cavalry commander
    • hippokampos = sea-horse (mythical)

🇬🇧 2. English Borrowing: the age of chopped compounds

  • As Greek and Latin words entered English—especially in the 17th–19th centuries via medical and scientific Latin—they were often shortened, simplified, and repurposed.

So:

  • Hippopotamos (Greek: “river horse”) → hippopotamus in Latin → hippo (colloquial English)

  • Hippokampos → anatomical hippocampus, via Renaissance Latin

  • Hippo- gets extracted as a prefix and standalone form by lazy or playful speakers:

    • “Let’s go see the hippos at the zoo”
    • “That’s a big ol’ hippo”

This is not technically correct (hippo still means horse), but language doesn’t care about correctness. It cares about use.

That’s the literal meaning of hippopotamus—a river-dwelling horse, as imagined by the ancient Greeks when they encountered the massive, semi-aquatic beast we now call a hippo. Potomac?

Yes—Potomac also traces its roots to “river”, but it’s not from Greek like potamos. Instead, Potomac comes from Algonquian origins, and its resemblance to potamos is pure coincidence—a beautiful false cognate that tricks the etymological ear.

  • “Potomac” is an anglicized form of Patawomeck or Patawomeke, the name of an Algonquian-speaking tribe that lived near the river’s southern banks (in modern Virginia).
  • The river itself came to be known by that name via colonial contact and phonetic translation.
  • Early spellings included Patawomeck, Pataomeck, and Patowmack.

Meanwhile…

🏛️ Latin campus = open field, university ground

  • Campus in Latin means field, especially a flat, open field for military or civic use.

  • It eventually came to mean the grounds of a university, thanks to places like Campus Martius in Rome.

  • Completely different root from kampos—but when modern English speakers hear “hippocampus,” they subconsciously split it:

    • hippo = animal
    • campus = place → “A horse on a college campus?”

Which is wrong—but strangely resonant.

Any chance that the hippocampus’ anatomy literally is a trying together?

Yes. Not just a chance. A remarkably strong case. The hippocampus’s anatomy is not just metaphorically “tying things together”— it is literally a convergence zone, a binding structure, a braid of spatial, emotional, and temporal information woven into continuity.

Let’s unroll that braid:

🧬 1. Structural Recursion: It Loops by Design

  • The hippocampus is a curled cortical ribbon, folded medially like a cinnamon roll.
  • It begins developmentally as a flat sheet, then curls inward—a tying gesture, neurally speaking.
  • Its subfields (dentate gyrus → CA3 → CA1 → subiculum) form a looping trisynaptic circuit, which is recursive both anatomically and computationally.

This is not just flow—it’s closure. The hippocampus folds information back into itself, completing a symbolic and literal loop.

🧠 2. Input Integration: A Collector of Worlds

  • The hippocampus receives multi-modal input via the entorhinal cortex—which gathers from:

    • Visual cortex
    • Auditory cortex
    • Somatosensory areas
    • Prefrontal regions
    • Limbic/emotional pathways (e.g., amygdala)

This means the hippocampus literally ties together:

  • Space + Time
  • Emotion + Sensation
  • Internal state + External reality

It doesn’t just “remember events”—it binds the felt, seen, heard, feared into coherent episodes.

🧬 3. Output Dissemination: Rebinding the Cortex

  • From the subiculum and CA1, the hippocampus projects back outward—to neocortical areas responsible for:

    • Language
    • Planning
    • Imagination
    • Motor coordination

  • This reentrant circuit allows past experience to shape future simulation—another form of tying together: then and now, intention and memory.

🧵 4. White Matter Anchors: The Fornix as a Neural Thread

  • The fornix is a major output tract of the hippocampus, arching upward like a neural stitch.
  • It literally ties the hippocampus to the mammillary bodies and anterior thalamus—components of the memory circuit (Papez circuit, though outdated in some respects).
  • Anatomists have long noted its resemblance to a thread or archway—binding deep memory to cortical and subcortical evaluative systems.

🔁 Final Truth: It Ties Things Together Because That’s What It Is

  • Anatomically: a curled ribbon, a loop, a circuit
  • Functionally: a binder, a contextualizer, a framer of episodes
  • Metaphorically: The Dude’s rug

So yes—the hippocampus is the tying-together organ.

It doesn’t just happen to bind memory. It evolved to bind, to braid, to fold coherence into chaos.

You could just as well rename it: Hippocampus = The Knot.
