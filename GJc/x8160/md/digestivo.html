🌀 Pericentral: Sensory, 3D#

Here we begin: the body-in-space. Primary motor and somatosensory cortices. Where the world enters not through reason, but through force: pressure, vibration, velocity. The world is 3D, before meaning—only intensity. This is the realm of facts without grammar. A slap, a caress, a plunge into cold water. No frame yet.

Signal without code. Raw unvectorized.

📡 Hippocampus: Reductionism, 2D#

From the somatic thrum, we carve out a map—2D, navigable, compressed. This is the grid cell dream of the rodent exploring a maze. The fantasy of flatness. The retina feeds a flattened world, and the hippocampus translates it into wayfinding, context, and sequence. The hippocampus is how the real Eiffel Tower and the souvenir collapse into the same node in your neural lattice.

The autoencoder flattens the real. A map is not the territory—but the hippocampus treats it like it is.

🐍 PFC: Association, ND (n-dimensional)#

Here lies ambiguity, recursion, abstraction. This is symbolic space, the territory of non-Euclidean meaning. The prefrontal cortex doesn’t just guess what’s missing—it hallucinates what ought to be there, inferring agency, intent, cause, metaphor. This is where Gestalt kicks in fully: closure, continuity, figure-ground reversal.

The mind abhors a vacuum—so it fills in the gaps with gods, intentions, and Eiffel Towers.

🗼 DMN: Figurative, 3D#

This is the dream tower. The figurative reconstruction of what the senses whispered and the PFC embellished. This is the realm of autobiographical simulation—the Eiffel Tower not as metal and rivet but as proposal site, postcard, war memory, cinematic backdrop. The DMN is where world and self braid together in narrative viscosity. It feels 3D, but it is internally rendered, a third-person omniscient hallucination.

This is where meaning lives. But it’s a lie. A beautiful one.

🔁 Cingulo-Insular: Memory, 2D#

And finally, the metacognitive spine, the salience switchboard, the pain and interoception observer. The cingulo-insular region doesn’t care about truth or simulation—it watches for conflict, uncertainty, cost. It’s the overseer of prediction error. The place where meaning can collapse, forcing re-encoding. You drop the souvenir tower and realize, in the shatter, that it never was Paris. You were never there. But your memory insists otherwise.

It stitches coherence with pain and resolve. Memory is a moral act.

What seems real is a successful fantasy, and the souvenir may be more real than the tower.

Can we propensity weight when our goal is personalization?

Yes—but only if you’re ruthless about what you’re weighting for. Propensity weighting is a means, not an end. If your goal is personalization—not generalization—you must invert the usual logic and use propensity scores as a tool of epistemic filtration, not averaging.

Let me break it down in Ukubona terms:

🔁 The Usual Propensity Setup (Population-Level Inference)#

  • Goal: estimate causal effect of treatment A vs. B by balancing covariates

  • Method: compute a propensity score (P[Treatment = 1 | X])

  • Then: weight, match, or stratify to simulate a randomized experiment

  • Outcome: population-level causal estimate (ATE, ATT, etc.)

This approach is concerned with the aggregate truth, the centroid. But personalization is not about the centroid—it’s about finding where you diverge from it.

🧬 Personalization Reframed#

When your goal is individualized inference, you don’t care about the average treatment effect. You care about:

“What would have happened to me or someone like me under a different world?”

So, instead of balancing populations, you use propensity logic to:

  • Identify local neighborhoods in covariate space

  • Reweight large samples to amplify the relevance of nearby cases

  • Downweight irrelevant cases that are epidemiologically distant

This is not weighting to remove bias—it’s weighting to amplify signal.

🛠 How to Use Propensity for Personalization:#

  1. Compute Propensity Scores for each unit’s probability of being in Pop A vs. Pop B (This gives you a latent similarity metric: how much this individual “belongs” in one world or the other)

  2. Invert or transform the propensity to reflect epistemic closeness to the target individual (e.g., personalize for a 68-year-old woman with mild frailty—then upweight data from similar profiles)

  3. Apply these weights in your simulation engine to:

    • Estimate counterfactual outcomes under the wrong population

    • Sharpen the variance estimation (posterior distributions, CI widths)

    • Plot survival or outcome distributions for that individual’s shadow cohort

🧠 Epistemic Punchline#

Personalization doesn’t mean ignoring population structure—it means reconstructing population evidence around the individual’s epistemic center.

So yes, you can and should use propensity scores—but not for neutrality. For epistemic alignment. That’s the Ukubona way: see from where you are, not from where the average stands.
